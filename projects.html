AI-Generated News and Social Media Trust

Examining how different forms of AI participation in news production shape trust, verification, and belief in social-media environments.

What I am studying

This project compares four news-production modes: human-led, AI-assisted, AI draft with human editing, and AI-dominant production. It asks whether readers respond differently when AI plays a larger role, and whether perceived human oversight changes how they evaluate an article, its publisher, and the need to verify its claims.

Research approach

I use OASIS to build controlled multi-agent social-media simulations. Heterogeneous agents encounter standardized news materials, receive production-mode disclosures, and make structured judgments about article quality, trust, human control, machine cues, reading, sharing, and verification. The design also tracks how these responses evolve across repeated exposure rather than relying on a single static judgment.

False-news experiment

A paired experiment tests the illusory truth effect by comparing repeated false statements with previously unseen false statements after two news exposures. The latest results show a stable repetition effect across all four production modes: repeated claims are consistently judged as more credible than novel claims.

AI-dominant production produces stronger negative machine heuristics, greater verification awareness, and lower overall trust in false statements. However, this skepticism applies to both repeated and novel claims, so it does not clearly eliminate the additional credibility created by repetition. The project therefore distinguishes a broad skepticism effect from a specific reduction in the illusory truth effect.

Current direction

I am extending the analysis across exposure intervals and individual news items, while separating the effects of production disclosure, memory accessibility, repeated exposure, and perceived human oversight. The next stage will use models that account for both agent-level and article-level variation.

Power Politics and Normative Dynamics in China’s Territorial Disputes

Building a longitudinal database to study how diplomatic engagement and political narratives develop around China’s territorial disputes.

What I am building

The project transforms multi-country news coverage into structured records of diplomatic activity. Each record captures relevant actors and institutions, visit direction, event type, policy topic, and temporal information, making it possible to compare diplomatic patterns across countries and over time.

Methods

I combine rule-based extraction with BERT-based text classification. The workflow includes source collection, event detection, entity normalization, annotation, classification, and reproducible quality checks. Particular attention is given to resolving alternative names for countries, officials, and institutions so that the resulting data remain consistent across sources.

Research goal

The database is designed to support analysis of how material power, diplomatic interaction, and normative language jointly shape territorial-dispute politics. It also provides a reusable foundation for studying changes in bilateral engagement and issue framing through large-scale political text data.
