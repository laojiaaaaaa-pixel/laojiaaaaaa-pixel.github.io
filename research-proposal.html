Research Proposal

Value Tendencies in Large Language Models and Their Social Consequences

Research Interests

Large language models are rapidly becoming an important channel through which people obtain information. Increasingly, instead of comparing multiple news sources or searching for information themselves, people simply ask AI systems to explain events, summarize controversies, or provide recommendations. As AI becomes an intermediary between humans and information, I became interested in a question that extends beyond model performance:

How might the value tendencies embedded in different large language models influence the way people understand the world?

This question did not originate from my AI research, but from my training in International Relations. During my studies, I was often struck by the fact that countries could reach completely different policy conclusions while looking at the same facts. Whether discussing territorial disputes, climate governance, or emerging technologies, disagreements rarely resulted from insufficient information. Instead, they reflected different normative assumptions and value priorities that shaped how information was interpreted.

This observation led me to think about today’s AI systems. If people increasingly rely on large language models to interpret complex social and political issues, then AI is no longer merely retrieving information—it is also participating in the interpretation of information. Existing studies have shown that different LLMs may exhibit different value tendencies, but I believe the more important question is what happens after these differences reach millions of users.

My current research has further strengthened this interest. I am developing an agent-based simulation of online information ecosystems to study how different forms of human–AI collaboration influence trust, information diffusion, and collective opinion formation. While building this system, I gradually realized that measuring differences between models is only the first step. What ultimately matters is whether these differences accumulate through repeated human–AI interactions and eventually reshape information ecosystems.

Answering this question requires more than evaluating language models. It requires connecting natural language processing, human behaviour, and computational social science. I hope to combine LLM analysis, behavioural experiments, and agent-based modelling to study how individual interactions with AI gradually evolve into collective social outcomes. Rather than viewing AI as an isolated technical system, I hope to understand it as a social actor whose embedded value tendencies may increasingly shape public opinion and the information environments in which people make decisions.

My background in International Relations enables me to identify socially meaningful questions, while my experience in NLP, large-scale text analysis, and agent-based modelling allows me to investigate these questions using computational methods. I hope to contribute this interdisciplinary perspective to research in social computing and to connect advances in AI with the broader societal transformations they may bring.
